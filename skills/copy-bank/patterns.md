# Hook patterns

Structures that have carried real campaigns. The famous examples are here to show the shape. They are other companies' taglines, many of them trademarked, so they're never used on a site, not even with a word swapped. "Ours" rows are lines an owner approved (see `approved.md`).

Awareness levels are from `SKILL.md` §3: **U** unaware, **P** problem-aware, **S** solution-aware, **Pr** product-aware, **M** most aware.

Read each pattern's **Fails when** before you use it. Most bad hooks are a good pattern used on the wrong reader.

---

## 1. Situation → payoff

`[Their situation, as a question or a fact] → [what they get, in an everyday phrase]`

- Got Milk? (California Milk Processor Board, 1993). The question works because the reader is already in the moment: a mouthful of cookie and an empty carton.
- Ours: Got a nice ride? → Let's show it off. / Got a listing? → Let it sell itself.

**Levels:** U, P. **Works when** the situation is one they're proud of or in right now, and the payoff is short. **Fails when** it stops at the question ("Got a bar?"), or when the situation is the problem itself and nothing follows ("You post. → The week still goes quiet.").

## 2. Name the moment

`[A scene from their day, so specific they recognize it]`, often paired with the product.

- Have a break, have a Kit Kat (Rowntree, 1957).
- You're not you when you're hungry (Snickers, 2010).

**Levels:** U. **Works when** the scene comes from §2 buyer words. **Fails when** it describes the footage ("the sound of the room") instead of the owner's moment, or the scene is generic ("busy day?").

## 3. Outcome, plainly

`[The result they want, stated as a promise people already say]`

- When it absolutely, positively has to be there overnight (Federal Express, 1978).
- The Ultimate Driving Machine (BMW, 1974).
- Ours: Let it sell itself. / Let's put it on the map.

**Levels:** P, S. **Works when** the outcome is the one every buyer wants and it's said the way they'd say it. **Fails when** it's a narrow mechanism ("Let them walk it from their phone") or a result the client can't stand behind ("double your sales").

## 4. You do yours, we do ours

`[What the owner keeps doing] → [what we take off their plate]`

- You press the button, we do the rest (Kodak, 1888).

**Levels:** P. **Works when** the buyer words are about time and exhaustion ("I'm managing everything on my own"), and the hand-off is something the client actually does. **Fails when** the owner's half is insulting ("You just show up") or vague.

## 5. Own the size

`[Admit the thing a bigger competitor would hide] → [why it's better for the buyer]`

- We're No. 2. We try harder (Avis, 1962).

**Levels:** S, Pr. **Works when** the buyer is comparing agencies and the client is small or new. **Fails when** it reads as an apology, or the "better" half has no proof.

## 6. The buyer's own words

`[A phrase lifted from a review, thread or DM, lightly trimmed]`

- If you think you need rehab, you do. Copyhackers took the line from a customer's book review and tested it against "Your addiction ends here"; [CXL reports](https://cxl.com/how-to-write-compelling-copy/) the review line won by a wide margin. Single secondary source.

**Levels:** P. **Works when** §2 has a quote that names the problem better than you could. This pattern is the reason §2 exists. **Fails when** the quote is private, identifies a person, or is long; keep it short and generic enough to be anyone's.

## 7. Who it's for

`[Name the buyer or the group they want to belong to]`

- Breakfast of Champions (Wheaties, 1930s).
- Belong anywhere (Airbnb, 2014).

**Levels:** U, P. **Works when** the buyer is proud of the group ("the Valley's small businesses", "the shops people line up for"). **Fails when** it flatters with a label they'd never use about themselves ("visionaries", "brands ready to stand out").

## 8. One concrete detail as proof

`[A single specific, checkable detail that makes the claim believable]`

- At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock (Ogilvy, 1958).

**Levels:** S, Pr. Better for subheads, service cards and the About section than the hero. **Works when** the detail is true and visible on the page (a reel, a count a visitor can check). **Fails when** the detail is invented, or it's a statistic from a blog.

## 9. A true number

`[A number that turns the benefit into something you can picture]`

- 1,000 songs in your pocket (Apple iPod, 2001).

**Levels:** S, Pr. **Works when** the number is real and in the facts file ("18 reels on this page"). **Fails when** it's rounded up, estimated, borrowed from the template or from a competitor.

## 10. A twist on a phrase they already say

`[An everyday phrase or idiom, bent to fit the offer]`

- Shave time. Shave money. (Dollar Shave Club, 2012)
- Maybe she's born with it. Maybe it's Maybelline. (1991)
- Ours (Spanish): Que se venda sola.

**Levels:** U, P. **Works when** the phrase is common in the site's language and region and the twist needs no explaining. **Fails when** it's a pun only a copywriter enjoys, or forced slang ("Got a spot?") that tries too hard to sound local.

## 11. Place pride

`[The place, said the way locals say it]`

- What happens here, stays here (Las Vegas, 2003).
- Ours: Serving the Valley.

**Levels:** U to Pr. Strong as a section head or a trust line in a local market. **Works when** the client is actually from there and works there. **Fails when** it's the only idea on the page, or uses a nickname locals don't use.

## 12. The invitation

`[Let's / an imperative, toward what they want]`

- Just do it (Nike, 1988).
- Ours: Let's put it on the map. / Let's show it off.

**Levels:** P, S. A good payoff half for pattern 1. **Works when** the verb is everyday and the object is theirs ("it" = their place, their ride, their listing). **Fails when** it's a command to work harder or a hype verb ("Dominate", "Elevate").

## 13. Take the risk away

`[A guarantee or a low-risk first step]`

- 30 minutes or it's free (Domino's, 1980s; dropped in 1993).

**Levels:** Pr, M. Best on the CTA, the pricing block and the signup. **Works when** the owner actually offers it (a free quote, month to month, a first shoot). **Fails when** nobody approved the guarantee. A guarantee is a fact: it goes in the facts file or `packages.md` first.

## 14. Against the usual way

`[What buyers are used to getting] → [what this business gives instead]`

- Melts in your mouth, not in your hands (M&M's, 1954).
- Most agencies sell reports. We sell revenue. (WeSell It, a reference brand, read 2026-11-03)

**Levels:** S. **Works when** the buyer has been burned (§2 has the quotes) and the client can prove the difference. **Fails when** it slides into the "not X, it's Y" AI tell, when there's no proof, or when it insults the reader's last choice. Use it once per page at most.

## 15. The feeling

`[What owning it says about them, or how it feels]`

- Because I'm worth it (L'Oréal, 1973).
- When you care enough to send the very best (Hallmark, 1944).

**Levels:** U. **Works when** the feeling is pride the owner already has (their place, their craft, their ride). **Fails when** it's an abstract feeling with no object ("Feel the difference").

## 16. The friendly tease

`[A small, true joke about the buyer's situation, told from their side]`

- You're not you when you're hungry (Snickers, 2010) teases the reader and makes them the hero of the joke.

**Levels:** P. **Works when** it's affectionate and the reader is in on it (the cousins liking every post). **Fails when** the reader is the butt of the joke, it scolds ("Your page looks dead"), or it needs local slang to land.

## 17. The plain label

`[A confident name for the section]`

- Ours: What we do. / Our work. / Serving the Valley.

**For:** section headings, nav and cards. Never a stunt, a question or an instruction ("Tap a reel to watch it on Instagram."). Service cards use the service's real name ("Video & Reels"), as a buyer would read it on a price sheet.

---

## Pairing on a hero slider

When the hero rotates several slides: slide 1 speaks to every visitor (patterns 4, 3, 7 or 15), and each later slide speaks to one kind of buyer, matching its photo or clip (pattern 1 with 12 as the payoff, or 2). Keep one structure across the slides so they read as a set.
