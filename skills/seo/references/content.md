# Keywords, clusters and content

## First: does this client need content at all?

Not every business does. A single-location juice bar with an unclaimed Google Business Profile should not be writing blog posts — the map pack work in `local.md` will out-earn a content program many times over.

Content is the right investment when the business sells something researched before it's bought, serves a wide area or sells online, competes on expertise, or needs to be the source AI assistants quote.

Saying "you don't need this yet" is worth more to the relationship than selling a content retainer that won't pay back.

---

## Keyword research without paid tools

If the client has Ahrefs or Semrush, use it. If not — and they usually don't — these produce real data rather than guesses:

- **Google Search Console.** The most undervalued source. Queries the site already gets impressions for, especially positions 11–30, are the fastest wins available: the page already ranks, it just isn't on page one.
- **Google autocomplete and "People also ask"** — actual query phrasing.
- **"Searches related to"** at the bottom of the results page.
- **The client's own inbox, DMs and phone calls.** What customers actually ask, in their words. Better than any tool.
- **Reviews** — competitors' too. People describe what they wanted in the language they'd search with.
- **Competitor sitemaps** — `/sitemap.xml` shows every topic a competitor decided was worth a page.

**Do not invent volumes.** If there's no data source, rank by judgment and say so: "no volume data — prioritized by business value and observed customer language." An invented number gets planned against.

## Clustering

One cluster = one page. Two pages targeting the same intent compete with each other, and Google picks one — usually the weaker.

Group by **search intent**, not by wording. "how to store cold pressed juice" and "how long does cold pressed juice last" are one page. "cold pressed juice near me" is a different page, because the intent is to buy, not to learn.

The test: would one page satisfy both searchers? Same cluster. Would the results pages look different? Different clusters.

**Structure**

```
Pillar — broad topic, one comprehensive page
├── Cluster page — subtopic
├── Cluster page — subtopic
└── Cluster page — subtopic
```

Every cluster page links up to the pillar; the pillar links down to all of them. That reciprocal linking is what makes the topical authority signal work — see `links.md`.

**Rules**

- 3–15 keywords per cluster. Fewer, merge it. More, split it.
- Every cluster needs one primary term.
- Comparisons (`X vs Y`) are always their own cluster.
- Location terms cluster by location, one page per location.
- Branded terms get their own cluster.

**Intent → format**, and getting this wrong is why pages fail:

| Intent | Query shape | Page |
|---|---|---|
| Informational | "what is", "how to", "why" | Guide, tutorial, FAQ |
| Commercial | "best", "top", "vs", "review" | Comparison, listicle |
| Transactional | "buy", "price", "near me", "order" | Product, service, or location page |
| Navigational | brand terms | Homepage, about, contact |

A blog post will never rank for a transactional query, however good it is.

## Cannibalization

Two pages competing for one intent is one of the most common problems on an established site and one of the easiest to fix.

Find it: `site:example.com [keyword]` in Google, or Search Console — one query whose landing page keeps changing is the signature.

Fix by merging the weaker into the stronger and 301-ing it, or by deliberately re-pointing one at a different intent. Don't leave both.

## Content brief

Before writing anything:

- **Primary keyword** and the intent behind it
- **Secondary terms** to cover naturally
- **Who it's for** and what they already know
- **Format**, matched to intent
- **Length**, set by what ranking pages actually do — not a round number
- **Outline** as question-shaped H2s
- **The answer**, in the first two or three sentences
- **FAQ** — 6–10 real questions, 40–60 word answers
- **Internal links** out, with the anchor text, and which existing pages should link in
- **Schema type**
- **The differentiator** — what this page has that page one doesn't. Original data, real photos, first-hand experience, a genuine local angle. Without one, there's no reason for it to outrank anything.

---

## Writing

- **Answer first.** Then context. Burying the answer costs both the featured snippet and the AI citation.
- **Question headings.** `## How long does cold-pressed juice last?`
- **Self-contained sections** — each should survive being lifted out alone.
- **Concrete over abstract.** "Pressed twice daily in a hydraulic press" beats "we're passionate about freshness." Specifics get quoted; atmosphere gets discarded.
- **No keyword stuffing.** Write it for the reader, then check the term appears naturally in the title, H1, first paragraph and a heading or two.
- **First-hand experience** — the first E in E-E-A-T, and the thing AI-generated competitors can't fake. Real photos, real numbers, things you can only know by doing it.
- **Named author with a real bio page.**
- **Visible published and updated dates.**

## Updating beats publishing

For a site with existing content, updating usually returns more than adding. Priority order:

1. Positions 11–30 for valuable queries — closest to a real gain
2. High impressions, low CTR — a title and description rewrite, no new content needed
3. Traffic declining year over year — stale, overtaken
4. Thin pages that could merge into something substantial

Update means genuinely improving it. Changing the date on unchanged content is a trick that stopped working and damages trust when noticed.
