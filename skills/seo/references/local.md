# Local SEO — the map pack

For a business with a physical location or a service area, this is where the money is. Someone searching "juice bar near me" or "best açaí Brownsville" gets a map with three results above the organic links. Being one of those three beats ranking #1 organically.

The local pack is ranked on three factors, roughly in this order:

1. **Relevance** — does the profile match the query
2. **Distance** — from the searcher, which you cannot control
3. **Prominence** — reviews, citations, links, brand mentions

You optimize relevance and prominence. Distance is why a single location can't rank city-wide, and why promising that is dishonest.

---

## Google Business Profile

The GBP *is* the homepage for local search. More people see it than the website. Treat it as the primary asset.

**Claim and verify.** Nothing works until verified. Video verification is now common — the owner walks the premises showing signage, equipment and the address.

**Complete every field.** Completeness itself is a ranking factor, and empty fields get filled by user suggestions you didn't approve.

- **Name** — the real-world business name exactly. No keyword stuffing ("Feel Good — Best Juice Bar Brownsville" is a violation and a competitor can report it).
- **Primary category** — the single highest-leverage field on the profile. Pick the most specific accurate one ("Juice Shop" over "Restaurant"). Check what the top three competitors use.
- **Secondary categories** — every one that genuinely applies. Each opens new queries.
- **Hours**, including holiday hours. Wrong hours generate one-star reviews from people who drove over.
- **Attributes** — the ones that match how people filter. For food: vegan, gluten-free, outdoor seating, wheelchair accessible, curbside pickup. These surface the business in filtered searches.
- **Description** — 750 characters, what you do and who for, written for humans.
- **Products / Services / Menu** — populate them. Menu items are indexed and match dish-level queries.
- **Booking and ordering links.**

**Photos.** Weekly beats a hundred at once. Real photos, geotagged if possible, showing food, interior, exterior with signage, and staff. The exterior shot matters more than people expect — it's how someone confirms they've arrived.

**Posts.** Weekly. Offers, events, new items. They decay in about seven days, which is the point — activity is a signal.

**Q&A.** Seed it yourself with the real questions people ask, then answer them. If you leave it empty, strangers answer for you, wrongly.

**Messaging.** Only enable it if someone will actually reply. Slow replies are penalized and irritate customers.

## NAP consistency

**N**ame, **A**ddress, **P**hone — identical everywhere on the internet. Google cross-references citations to decide the business is real and its details are correct. Variants split that confidence.

Audit these at minimum: Google Business Profile, Apple Business Connect, Bing Places, Yelp, Facebook, Instagram bio, the website footer, `LocalBusiness` schema, delivery platforms (DoorDash, Uber Eats, Grubhub), the local chamber of commerce, the CVB or tourism board, and any industry directory.

Standardize the exact string first — including suite formatting and whether it's "St" or "Street" — then fix every listing to match.

Watch for these, all of which are common and all of which cost rankings:

- A previous business name still listed (a rebrand leaves the old name everywhere for years)
- A dead website URL on an authoritative listing
- Suite number present in some listings, absent in others, or written differently
- A tracking phone number that differs from the real one
- Duplicate profiles — merge or remove them; duplicates split reviews and confuse ranking

Apple Maps is worth its own mention: it powers Siri and iOS Maps, it is separate from Google, and most small businesses have never claimed it.

## Reviews

The strongest prominence signal, and the one most businesses handle passively.

**Velocity matters more than total.** Twenty reviews earned steadily over a year beats sixty in one week, which looks bought and can trigger a filter.

**Build a system, not a campaign.** A QR code at the counter, a link in the receipt or order confirmation, staff trained to ask at the natural moment. Ask everyone.

**Never gate.** Screening for happy customers before asking is an FTC violation in the US and against Google's policy. Ask everyone, then fix what the bad ones tell you.

**Respond to all of them**, positive and negative, quickly, in the owner's voice. Responses are public and read by prospects. A calm reply to a bad review sells better than the review costs.

**Review content is indexed.** Reviews mentioning specific dishes help those dishes rank. You can't script that, but you can ask an open question — "what did you order?" — rather than "please leave five stars".

Do not buy reviews. It is detectable, removable, and in the US it is an FTC enforcement matter.

## The website's local layer

- **`LocalBusiness` schema** with the most specific type available (`Restaurant`, `CafeOrCoffeeShop`, `HealthAndBeautyBusiness`). Include `geo` coordinates, `openingHoursSpecification`, `sameAs` for every social profile, `hasMenu` where relevant, and `priceRange`. Templates in `schema.md`.
- **NAP in crawlable HTML**, not baked into an image. Footer on every page.
- **An embedded map** on the contact or visit page.
- **The city and region in title tags** on the pages where it's natural, without stuffing it into every one.
- **One page per location** if there's more than one — never a single page listing them all.
- **Service-area pages** for businesses that travel to customers, one per real area, each with genuinely different content. Boilerplate pages with the city name swapped are doorway pages, and Google treats them as spam.
- **Local content that a national competitor can't write** — the neighborhood, nearby landmarks, local events, partnerships with other local businesses. This is also what earns local links.

## Local links and citations

Ordinary link building is hard. Local link building is easy and nobody does it.

- Chamber of commerce
- Tourism board / CVB listing
- Local news — a new location, a new menu, a seasonal item, an owner story
- Sponsorships: a youth team, a school event, a 5K
- Local blogs and "best of" roundups
- Nearby non-competing businesses — cross-links between a gym and a juice bar are natural
- Industry-specific directories, real ones only, never bulk submission services
- University and college pages if there's a student discount

## Measuring local

From GBP Insights / Performance:

- Searches split into **discovery** (found by category) vs **direct** (searched the name). Discovery growth means new customers; direct growth means brand awareness.
- Calls, direction requests, website clicks, message volume
- Photo views against competitors
- Which search terms surfaced the profile

Track ranking by distance, not as one number. A local rank tracker showing a grid across the service area tells you the real story: a business typically ranks well at its own pin and fades within a couple of miles. That fade is the thing to work on.

---

## Order of work

1. Claim and verify the profile
2. Fix the primary category, then secondary categories
3. Fill every field
4. Fix NAP everywhere
5. Start the review system
6. Add `LocalBusiness` schema and crawlable NAP to the site
7. Photos and posts, weekly, ongoing
8. Local links

Steps 1–5 are usually a week of work and outperform six months of blogging for a business like this. Do them first.
